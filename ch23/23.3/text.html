Chemical Potential of Two Phases in Equilibrium

Equilibrium between two phases, e.g. water ice ($ice$) and liquid water ($water$), is at constant $\kT$ and $\kP$. Therefore:

As $d\knwater$ is not zero, this means that $\kDeltamu$ must be zero! This must hold true for any set of points where ice and water are in equilibrium. The statement is not just for liquid and solid water, but for any two phases in equilibrium. That is, any two phases in equilbrium will always have the same chemical potential.

The Clapeyron Equation

That is the almost vertical line in the diagram. Its points are not at the same $\kP$ and $\kT$, but we can find out where they should be by considering the thermodynamic potential $\kmu$ as a function of $\kT$ and $\kP$:

This expression should be valid for all points along a phase boundary, such as the melt line. In fact, it tells use that the phase boundary is defined by $\kDeltaHbar/\kT\kDeltaVbar$. For water and ice, we immediately see why the melt line runs a little to the left: exceptionally $\kDeltaVbar$ is negative for ice, because water is actually a little denser that ice.

The above expression(s) are named after Clapeyron. The values of $\kDeltaHbar$ and $\kDeltaVbar$ do not change much with pressure and can often be considered constants for the melting line. When gases are involved that is not really true.
