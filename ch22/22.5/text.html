Four equations in their natural variables

The fundamental thermodynamic equations follow from five primary thermodynamic definitions and describe internal energy, enthalpy, Helmholtz energy, and Gibbs energy in terms of their natural variables. Here they will be presented in their differential forms.

Introduction

The fundamental thermodynamic equations describe the thermodynamic quantities U, H, G, and A in terms of their natural variables. The term "natural variable" simply denotes a variable that is one of the convenient variables to describe U, H, G, or A. When considered as a whole, the four fundamental equations demonstrate how four important thermodynamic quantities depend on variables that can be controlled and measured experimentally. Thus, they are essentially equations of state, and using the fundamental equations, experimental data can be used to determine sought-after quantities like $\kG$ or $\kHenth$.

First Law of Thermodynamics

The first law of thermodynamics is represented below in its differential form

$$ d\kU = đ\kq+đ\kw $$

where

  • $\kU$ is the internal energy of the system,
  • $\kq$ is heat flow of the system, and
  • $\kw$ is the work of the system.

The "đ" symbol represent inexact differentials and indicates that both $\kq$ and $\kw$ are path functions. Recall that $\kU$ is a state function. The first law states that internal energy changes occur only as a result of heat flow and work done.

It is assumed that w refers only to PV work, where

$$ \kw = -\int{\kp d\kV} $$

The fundamental thermodynamic equation for internal energy follows directly from the first law and the principle of Clausius:

$$ d\kU = đ\kq + đ\kw $$ $$ d\kSent = \dfrac{\delta \kqrev}{\kT} $$

we have

$$ d\kU = \kT d\kSent + \delta \kw $$

Since only $PV$ work is performed,

$$ d\kU = \kT d\kSent - \kp d\kV \tag{22.5.1} $$

The above equation is the fundamental equation for $\kU$ with natural variables of entropy $\kSent$ and volume$\kV$.

Principle of Clausius

The Principle of Clausius states that the entropy change of a system is equal to the ratio of heat flow in a reversible process to the temperature at which the process occurs. Mathematically this is written as

$$ d\kSent = \dfrac{\delta \kqrev}{\kT} $$

where

  • $\kSent$ is the entropy of the system,
  • $\kqrev$ is the heat flow of a reversible process, and
  • $\kT$ is the temperature in Kelvin.

Enthalpy

Mathematically, enthalpy is defined as

$$ \kHenth = \kU + \kp\kV \tag{22.5.2} $$

where $\kHenth$ is enthalpy of the system, $\kp$ is pressure, and $\kV$ is volume. The fundamental thermodynamic equation for enthalpy follows directly from it deffinition (Equation 22.5.2) and the fundamental equation for internal energy (Equation 22.5.1) :

$$ \begin{aligned} d\kHenth &= d\kU + d(\kp\kV) \\[4pt] &= d\kU + \kp d\kV + \kV d\kP \\[4pt] d\kU &= \kT d\kSent - \kp d\kV \\[4pt] d\kHenth &= \kT d\kSent - \kp d\kV + \kp d\kV + \kV d\kp \\[4pt] d\kHenth &= \kT d\kSent + \kV d\kp \end{aligned} $$

The above equation is the fundamental equation for $\kHenth$. The natural variables of enthalpy are $\kSent$ and $\kp$, entropy and pressure.

Gibbs Energy

The mathematical description of Gibbs energy is as follows

$$ \begin{aligned} \kG &= \kU + \kp\kV - \kT\kSent \\[4pt] &= \kHenth - \kT\kSent \end{aligned} \tag{22.5.3} $$

where $\kG$ is the Gibbs energy of the system. The fundamental thermodynamic equation for Gibbs Energy follows directly from its definition 22.5.3 and the fundamental equation for enthalpy 22.5.2:

$$ \begin{aligned} d\kG &= d\kHenth - d(\kT\kSent) \\[4pt] & d\kHenth - \kT d\kSent - \kSent d\kT \end{aligned} $$

Since

$$ \begin{aligned} d\kHenth &= \kT d\kSent + \kV d\kP \\[4pt] d\kG &= \kT d\kSent + \kV d\kP - \kT d\kSent - \kSent d\kT \\[4pt] &= \kV d\kP - \kSent d\kT \end{aligned} \tag{22.5.4} $$

The above equation is the fundamental equation for $\kG$. The natural variables of Gibbs energy are $\kP$ and $\kT$.

Helmholtz Energy

Mathematically, Helmholtz energy is defined as

$$ \kA = \kU - \kT\kSent \tag{22.5.5} $$

where $\kA$ is the Helmholtz energy of the system, which is often written as the symbol $F$. The fundamental thermodynamic equation for Helmholtz energy follows directly from its definition (Equation 22.5.5) and the fundamental equation for internal energy (Equation 22.5.1):

$$ \begin{aligned} d\kA &= d\kU - d(\kT\kSent) \\[4pt] &= d\kU - \kT d\kSent - \kSent d\kT \end{aligned} $$

Since

$$ \begin{aligned} d\kU &= \kT d\kSent - \kp d\kV \\[4pt] d\kA &= \kT d\kSent - \kp d\kV -\kT d\kSent - \kSent d\kT \\[4pt] &= -\kp d\kV - \kSent d\kT \end{aligned} \tag{22.5.6} $$

Equation 22.5.6 is the fundamental equation for $\kA$ with natural variables of $\kV$ and $\kT$. For the definitions to hold, it is assumed that only PV work is done and that only reversible processes are used. These assumptions are required for the first law and the principle of Clausius to remain valid. Also, these equations do not account include n, the number of moles, as a variable. When $n$ is included, the equations appear different, but the essence of their meaning is captured without including the n-dependence.

Chemical Potential

The fundamental equations derived above were not dependent on changes in the amounts of species in the system. Below the n-dependent forms are presented1,4.

$$ d\kU = \kT d\kSent - \kP d\kV + \sum_{i=1}^{N}\kmui dn_i $$ $$ d\kHenth = \kT d\kSent + \kV d\kP + \sum_{i=1}^{N}\kmui dn_i $$ $$ d\kG = -\kSent d\kT + \kV d\kp + \sum_{i=1}^{N}\kmui dn_i $$ $$ d\kA = -\kSent d\kT - \kP d\kV + \sum_{i=1}^{N}\kmui dn_i $$

where μi is the chemical potential of species i and dni is the change in number of moles of substance i.

Importance/Relevance of Fundamental Equations

The differential fundamental equations describe U, H, G, and A in terms of their natural variables. The natural variables become useful in understanding not only how thermodynamic quantities are related to each other, but also in analyzing relationships between measurable quantities (i.e. P, V, T) in order to learn about the thermodynamics of a system. Below is a table summarizing the natural variables for U, H, G, and A:

Table
Thermodynamic QuantityNatural Variables
U (internal energy)S, V
H (enthalpy)S, P
G (Gibbs energy)T, P
A (Helmholtz energy)T, V

Maxwell Relations

The fundamental thermodynamic equations are the means by which the Maxwell relations are derived1,4. The Maxwell Relations can, in turn, be used to group thermodynamic functions and relations into more general "families"2,3.

As we said dA is an exact differential. Let's write is out in its natural variables (Equation 22.5.6) and take a cross derivative. The dA expression in natural variables is

$$ d\kA = \left( \dfrac{\partial \kA}{\partial \kV} \right)_{\kT} d\kV + \left( \dfrac{\partial \kA}{\partial \kT} \right) _{\kV} d\kT $$

The partial derivatives of A of first order can already be quite interesting we see e.g. in step 2 that the first partial of A versus V (at T constant) is the negative of the pressure.

$$ \left( \dfrac{\partial \kA}{\partial \kV} \right)_{\kT} = -\kP $$

Likewise we find the (isochoric) slope with temperature gives us the negative of the entropy. Thus entropy is one of the first order derivatives of A.

$$ \left( \dfrac{\partial \kA}{\partial \kT} \right) _{\kV} = -\kSent $$
