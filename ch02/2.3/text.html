A negative separation constant

The boundary conditions for the string held to zero at both ends argue that $\ku(\kx,\kt)$ collapses to zero at the extremes of the string (Figure 2.3.1).

Unfortunately, when $K>0$, the general solution (Equation 2.2.7) results in a sum of exponential decays and growths that cannot achieve the boundary conditions (except for the trivial solution); hence $K<0$. This means we must introduce complex numbers due to the $\sqrt{K}$ terms in Equation 2.2.5. So we can rewrite $K$:

$$ K = - \kpn^2 \tag{2.3.1} $$

and Equation 2.2.4b can be

$$ \dfrac{d^2X(\kx)}{d\kx^2} +\kpn^2 X(\kx) = 0 \tag{2.3.2} $$

The general solution to differential equations of the form of Equation 2.3.2 is

$$ X(\kx) = A e^{i\kpn\kx} + B e^{-i\kpn\kx} \tag{2.3.3} $$

Quantization by boundary conditions

Example 2.3.1

Verify that Equation 2.3.3 is the general form for differential equations of the form of Equation 2.3.2, which when substituted with Equation 2.3.1 give

$$ X(\kx) = A e^{i\kpn\kx} + B e^{-i\kpn\kx} $$

Solution

Expand the complex exponentials into trigonometric functions via Euler formula ($e^{i \theta} = \cos \theta + i\sin \theta$)

Substituting Equations 2.3.10 and 2.3.6 into Equation 2.3.5 results in

$$ X(\kx) = c_2 \sin \left(\dfrac{n\pi \kx}{\kL} \right) $$

which can simplify to

$$ X(\kx) = c_2 \sin \left( \omega \kx \right) $$

with

$$ \omega=\dfrac{n\pi}{\kL} $$

A similar argument applies to the other half of the ansatz ($T(\kt)$).
