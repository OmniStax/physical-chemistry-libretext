The hydrogen atom's Hamiltonian

The hydrogen atom, consisting of an electron and a proton, is a two-particle system, and the internal motion of two particles around their center of mass is equivalent to the motion of a single particle with a reduced mass. This reduced particle is located at $\kr$, where $\kr$ is the vector specifying the position of the electron relative to the position of the proton. The length of $\kr$ is the distance between the proton and the electron, and the direction of $\kr$ and the direction of $\kr$ is given by the orientation of the vector pointing from the proton to the electron. Since the proton is much more massive than the electron, we will assume throughout this chapter that the reduced mass equals the electron mass and the proton is located at the center of mass.

Since the internal motion of any two-particle system can be represented by the motion of a single particle with a reduced mass, the description of the hydrogen atom has much in common with the description of a diatomic molecule discussed previously. The time-independent Schrödinger Equation for the hydrogen atom

$$ \kH (\kr , \ktheta , \kvarphi ) \kpsi (\kr , \ktheta , \kvarphi ) = \kE \kpsi ( \kr , \ktheta , \kvarphi) \tag{6.1.1} $$

employs the same kinetic energy operator, $\kThat$, written in spherical coordinates. For the hydrogen atom, however, the distance, $\kr$, between the two particles can vary, unlike the diatomic molecule where the bond length was fixed and the rigid rotor model was applicable. The hydrogen atom Hamiltonian also contains a potential energy term, $\kVhat$, to describe the attraction between the proton and the electron. This term is the Coulomb potential energy,

$$ \kVhat (\kr) = - \dfrac {\ke^2}{4 \pi \epsilon _0 \kr } \tag{6.1.2} $$

where r is the distance between the electron and the proton. The Coulomb potential energy depends inversely on the distance between the electron and the nucleus and does not depend on any angles. Such a potential is called a central potential.

The full expression for $\kH$ in spherical coordinates is

$$ \kH (\kr , \ktheta , \kvarphi ) = - \dfrac {\hbar ^2}{2 \kmu \kr^2} \left [ \dfrac {\partial}{\partial \kr} \left (\kr^2 \dfrac {\partial}{\partial \kr} \right ) + \dfrac {1}{\sin \ktheta } \dfrac {\partial}{\partial \ktheta } \left ( \sin \ktheta \dfrac {\partial}{\partial \ktheta} \right ) + \dfrac {1}{\sin ^2 \ktheta} \dfrac {\partial ^2}{\partial \kvarphi ^2} \right ] - \dfrac {\ke^2}{4 \pi \epsilon _0 \kr } \tag{6.1.3} $$

The angular momentum eigenvalues

Rearranging Equation 6.1.9 yields

$$ \kMsqhat \kYlm (\ktheta , \kvarphi ) = \klambda \kYlm (\ktheta , \kvarphi ) \tag{6.1.10} $$

where we have added the indices $l$ and $m_l$ to identify a particular spherical harmonic function. Note that the notation has changed from that used with the Rigid Rotor; it is customary to use $J$ and $m_J$ to represent the angular momentum quantum numbers for rotational states, but for electronic states, it is customary to use $l$ and $m_l$ to represent the same thing. Furthermore, the electronic angular momentum is designated by $L$ and the corresponding operator is called $\hat {L}$. In complete electronic notation, Equation 6.1.10 is

$$ \kLsqhat \kYlm (\ktheta , \kvarphi ) = \klambda \kYlm (\ktheta , \kvarphi ) \tag{6.1.11} $$

Equation 6.1.11 says that $\kYlm (\ktheta , \kvarphi )$ must be an eigenfunction of the angular momentum operator $\kLsqhat$ with eigenvalue λ.
