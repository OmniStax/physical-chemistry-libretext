The five principles

  1. The state of a quantum system is completely specified by its wavefunction $\ket{\kpsi}$.

  2. To every observable quantity in classical mechanics, there exists a Hermitian quantum mechanical operator. For example: kinetic energy in Classical Mechanics is

    $$ \text{kinetic energy} = \dfrac{p^2}{2\km} \tag{classical mechanics} $$

    and in Quantum Mechanics is

    $$ \text{kinetic energy} = \dfrac{\hat{p}^2}{2\km} = -\frac{\hbar}{2\km}\nabla^2. \tag{quantum mechanics} $$

  3. Observable quantities in quantum mechanics are eigenvalues of their corresponding operators: $\kAhat\ket{\phi_n} = A_n\ket{\phi_n}$.

  4. The set of eigenfunctions of an operator are orthogonal: $\braket{\phi_n|\phi_m} = \delta_{nm}$. The Kronecker delta $\delta_{nm}$ is 1 if $n = m$, and zero otherwise. As a secondary point, the set of all eigenfunctions of an operator form a complete set. So if $\ket{\phi_n}$ ($n = 0$, 1, 2 $\ldots$) are the set of eigenfunctions of an operator $\kAhat$, then any other state in the same space can be expressed as a linear combination of these functions:

    $$ \ket{\kpsi} = \sum_n c_n\ket{\phi_n}, $$

    where $c_n$ are complex numbers (the exact values are $c_n = \braket{\phi_n|\kpsi}$, as you can see by applying a projection operator on $\ket{\kpsi}$).

  5. If two operators do not commute, then their corresponding observables cannot be measured simultaneously to arbitrary precision. The most famous example is position and momentum:

    $$ [\hat{x},\kphat] = \hat{x}\kphat - \kphat\hat{x} = i\hbar, $$

    and as a result

    $$ \Delta p_x \Delta x \geq \hbar/2. $$
