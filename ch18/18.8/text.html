Principal axes and three kinds of top

For a polyatomic molecule containing $N$ atoms, the total number of degrees of freedom is $3N$. Out of these, three degrees of freedom are taken up for the translational motion of the molecule as a whole. For nonlinear molecules, there are three rotational degrees of freedom and the $3N - 6$ vibrational degrees. For linear molecules, the rotational motion along the molecular axis is quantum mechanically not meaningful as the rotated configuration is indistinguishable from the original configuration. Therefore, linear molecules have two rotational degrees of freedom and $3N - 5$ vibrational degrees of freedom.

To investigate the rotational motion, we need to fix the center of mass of the molecule and calculate the three principal moments of inertia $\kIA$, $\kIB$, and $\kIC$ of the ellipsoid of inertia. The center of mass is defined as the point for which the following identities hold:

$$ \sum_i \kmi x_i = \sum_i \kmi y_i = \sum_i \kmi z_i $$

The inertia products are defined by:

$$ \kIxx = \sum_i \kmi \left(y_i^2+ z^2_i\right) $$ $$ \kIxy = \sum_i \kmi \left(x_i y_i\right) $$

The other components $I_{yy}$, $I_{xz}$,.. are defined analogously. To find the direction cosines $\alpha_i, \beta_o, \gamma_i$ of the three principle moments of inertia, we need to solve the following matrix equations:

$$ \alpha (\kIxx - \eta) - \beta \kIxy - \gamma I_{xz} =0 $$ $$ \alpha \kIxy - \beta (I_{yy} - \eta) - \gamma I_{yz} = 0 $$ $$ -\alpha I_{xz} - \beta I_{yz} + \gamma (I_{zz} - \eta ) =0 $$

If the off diagonal terms $\kIxy$ are zero in the above equations, then the $x$, $y$, $z$ axis will be the principal axis. The energy of a rotor with the three moments of inertia $\kIA$, $\kIB$. and $\kIC$ is given by:

$$ \begin{aligned} \epsilon &= \dfrac{1}{2} \kIA \komegaA^2 + \dfrac{1}{2} \kIB \komegaB^2 + \dfrac{1}{2} \kIC \komegaC^2 \\[4pt] &= \dfrac{\kLA^2}{2\kIA} + \dfrac{\kLB^2}{2\kIB} + \dfrac{\kLC^2}{2\kIC} \end{aligned} $$

Each of the rotational degrees of freedom will have a characteristic rotational temperature in terms of the moment of inertia:

$$ \kThetaroti = \frac{hbar^2}{2\kIi k} \qquad i=A,B,C $$

There are many different shapes of molecules and these shapes affect the rotational behavior of the molecules. Molecules are therefore classified according to symmetry into different groups called tops. The three different tops are:

$$ \text{Spherical top} \qquad \Theta_{\text{rot},A} = \Theta_{\text{rot},B} = \Theta_{\text{rot},C} $$ $$ \text{Symmetric top} \qquad \Theta_{\text{rot},A} = \Theta_{\text{rot},B} \neq \Theta_{\text{rot},C} $$ $$ \text{Asymmetric top} \qquad \Theta_{\text{rot},A} \neq \Theta_{\text{rot},B} \neq \Theta_{\text{rot},C} $$

Symmetric Tops

Here, $\komegaA$, $\komegaB$, and $\komegaC$ are the three angular speeds and $\kLA$, $\kLB$, and $\kLC$ are the three angular momenta. For a symmetric top molecule such as ammonia, or chloromethane, two components of the moments of inertia are equal, i.e., $\kIB = \kIC$. The rotational energy levels of such a molecule are specified by two quantum numbers $J$ and $K$. The total angular momentum is determined by $J$ and the component of this angular momentum along the unique molecular axis is determined by $K$. The energy levels are given by:

$$ \kepsJK = \kBt J(J+1) + (\kAtilde - \kBt) K^2 $$

with rotational constants in units of wavenumbers:

$$ \kBt = \dfrac{h}{8\pi^2 c \kIB} $$

and:

$$ \kAtilde = \dfrac{h}{8\pi^2 c \kIA} $$

where

  • $J$ takes on values $0,1,2,.... \infty$ and
  • $K = -J, -J + 1, - J + 2,...0, 1, 2,...J$.

Asymmetric Tops

For asymmetric tops, the expressions for rotational energies are more complex and the conversions to integrations are not easy. One can actually calculate the sum of terms using a computer. An intuitive answer can be obtained by integrating over the angular momenta $\kLA$, $\kLB$ and $\kLC$ as:

$$ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{\kHclass(p,q)/k\kT} \,d\kLA \,d\kLB \,d\kLC = \sqrt{ 2\pi \kIA k\kT} \sqrt{ 2\pi \kIB k\kT} \sqrt{ 2\pi \kIC k\kT} $$

And then multiplying by a factor of $8 π^2 /σ h^3$, we get the rotational partition function. The factor of $8π^2$ accounts for the angular integration. For any axis chosen in a molecule, a complete rotation contributes a factor of $2π$. Integration over all possible orientations of this axis contribute another factor of $4π$. The factor of $h^3$ is for the conversion from the classical phase space to the quantum mechanical phase space.

Note

Symmetry Number

As discussed previously, the symmetry number $σ$ corrects for overcounting of rotational configurations. If the molecule has no center of symmetry (e.g. $\mathrm{HCN}$) $\sigma = 1$ whereas if the molecule has a center of symmetry (e.g. $\mathrm{CO_{2}}$) then $\sigma = 2$.

The final result is:

$$ \kqrot = \dfrac{\pi^2}{\sigma} \sqrt{\dfrac{8 \pi \kIA k\kT}{h^2}} \sqrt{\dfrac{8 \pi \kIB k\kT}{h^2}} \sqrt{\dfrac{8 \pi \kIC k\kT}{h^2}} \tag{18.8.1} $$
Note

Classical Derivation (Optional)

We can explicitly obtain the classical rotational partition function of an asymmetric top by writing the classical expression for the rotational energy in terms of the Euler angles. The orientation of a rigid rotor can be specified by there Euler angles $\ktheta$, $φ$, and $\kpsieul$ with the ranges of angles $0$ to $π$, $0$ to $2π$ and $0$ to $2π$ respectively. The rotational Hamiltonian for the kinetic energy can be written in terms of the angles and their conjugate momenta ( $\kptheta$, $\kpphi$, $\kppsi$)

We can simplify calculations by defining characteristic rotational temperatures for each axis of rotational:

$$ \kThetaA = \dfrac{h^2}{8 \pi^2 \kIA k} $$ $$ \kThetaB = \dfrac{h^2}{8 \pi^2 \kIB k} $$ $$ \kThetaC = \dfrac{h^2}{8 \pi^2 \kIC k} $$

The polyatomic rotational partition function expressed in Equation 18.8.1 can then be re-expressed as:

$$ \kqrot = \dfrac{\sqrt{\pi}}{\sigma}\sqrt{\dfrac{\kT^3}{ \kThetaA \kThetaB \kThetaC }} \tag{18.8.3} $$

or alternatively:

$$ \ln \kqrot = \dfrac{1}{2} \ln \dfrac{\pi \kT}{ \kThetaA \kThetaB \kThetaC \sigma^2} $$

Example · Nitrogen Dioxide

The three characteristic rotational temperatures for $\mathrm{NO_2}$ are $111.5\, K$, $0.624\, K$ and $0.590\, K$. Calculate the rotational partition function at 300 K.

Solution

The rotational temperature is given by Equation 18.8.3:

$$ \kqrot = \dfrac{\sqrt{\pi}}{\sigma} \sqrt{\dfrac{\kT^3}{ \kThetaA \kThetaB \kThetaC }} $$

The rotational partition function becomes,

$$ \kqrot = \dfrac{1.772}{2} \sqrt{\dfrac{300\; K}{ (11.5\; K)(0.624\,K) ( 22.55\;K)}} = 2242.4 $$
