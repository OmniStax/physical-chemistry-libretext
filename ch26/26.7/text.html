How K changes with temperature

We can use Gibbs-Helmholtz to get the temperature dependence of $K$

$$ \left( \dfrac{∂[\kDeltaGstd/\kT]}{∂\kT} \right)_P = \dfrac{-\kDeltaHstd}{\kT^2} $$

At equilibrium, we can equate $\kDeltaGstd$ to $-\kR\kT\ln K$ so we get:

$$ \left( \dfrac{∂[\ln K]}{∂\kT} \right)_P = \dfrac{\kDeltaHstd}{\kR\kT^2} $$

We see that whether $K$ increases or decreases with temperature is linked to whether the reaction enthalpy is positive or negative. If temperature is changed little enough that $\kDeltaHstd$ can be considered constant, we can translate a $K$ value at one temperature into another by integrating the above expression, we get a similar derivation as with melting point depression:

$$ \ln \dfrac{K(\kTtwo)}{K(\kTone)} = \dfrac{-\kDeltaHstd}{\kR} \left( \dfrac{1}{\kTtwo} - \dfrac{1}{\kTone} \right) $$

If more precision is required we could correct for the temperature changes of $\kDeltaHstd$ by using heat capacity data.

The van 't Hoff plot

The expression for $K$ is a rather sensitive function of temperature given its exponential dependence on the difference of stoichiometric coefficients One way to see the sensitive temperature dependence of equilibrium constants is to recall that

$$ K=e^{−\kDeltarGstd/\kR\kT} \tag{26.7.1} $$

However, since under constant pressure and temperature

$$ \kDeltaGstd= \kDeltaHstd−\kT\kDeltaSstd $$

Equation 26.7.1 becomes

$$ K=e^{-\kDeltaHstd/\kR\kT} e^{\kDeltaSstd/\kR} \tag{26.7.2} $$

Taking the natural log of both sides, we obtain a linear relation between $\ln K$and the standard enthalpies and entropies:

$$ \ln K = - \dfrac{\kDeltaHstd}{\kR} \dfrac{1}{\kT} + \dfrac{\kDeltaSstd}{\kR} \tag{26.7.3} $$

which is known as the van ’t Hoff equation. It shows that a plot of $\ln K$ vs. $1/\kT$ should be a line with slope $-\kDeltarHstd/\kR$ and intercept $\kDeltarSstd/\kR$.

Hence, these quantities can be determined from the $\ln K$ vs. $1/\kT$ data without doing calorimetry. Of course, the main assumption here is that $\kDeltarHstd$ and $\kDeltarSstd$ are only very weakly dependent on $\kT$, which is usually valid.
