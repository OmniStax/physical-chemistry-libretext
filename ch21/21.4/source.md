# The Debye Function is Used to Calculate the Heat Capacity at Low Temperatures

The Debye function is a mathematical expression used in solid-state physics to calculate the heat capacity of solids at low temperatures. Unlike the classical Dulong–Petit law, which assumes that each atom contributes a constant amount of heat capacity, the Debye model incorporates quantum mechanics by considering the vibrational modes of a crystal lattice as quantized phonons. At low temperatures, only the low-frequency vibrational modes are thermally accessible, causing the heat capacity to decrease sharply rather than remain constant. The Debye function provides a way to describe this temperature dependence, predicting the characteristic behavior at very low temperatures and approaching the classical limit at higher temperatures:

$$ C_P=aT^3 $$ {eq:1} n=1

Using quantum mechanics, Debye showed that the vibrational energy of a crystal lattice could be modeled as a collection of quantized normal modes, or phonons, leading to a temperature-dependent heat capacity that accurately describes low-temperature behavior in Equation [ref:eq:1].

However, for metallic crystals, the situation is slightly more complex. In addition to lattice vibrations, the conduction electrons contribute to the heat capacity, resulting in a modified equation at low temperatures. The total heat capacity $C(T)$ for metals is often expressed as:

$$ C_P=aT+bT^3 $$ {eq:2} n=2

where $a$ and $b$ are constants.

The $aT$ term in Equation [ref:eq:2] represents the linear contribution from conduction electrons, while the $bT^3$ term arises from the lattice vibrations described by the Debye model (form Equation [ref:eq:1]. This combination allows for an accurate prediction of the low-temperature heat capacity of metals.
